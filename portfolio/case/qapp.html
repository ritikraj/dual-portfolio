the defects found too late problem

defects were being found at final inspection, after the shipment was already made. q-app moved the check onto the stitching line, on a tablet a stitching master can use without putting the cloth down.

the problem

workmanship defects surfaced at shipment, when they cost the most to fix.

what i did

a line-side app where passing a piece is one tap, and a defect costs a piece number.

how it landed

workmanship defects at final inspection fell by roughly 30 to 40%.

the floor

q-app runs in factories of every size, from two or three stitching lines to forty, in places big enough that you take a small caddy from one department to the next. the network comes and goes. the people on the line are literate, but often in neither english nor hindi.

and the stitching master checking each piece is using both hands. every decision below comes from that.

four decisions, all from the floor

  1. the posture decides the deviceboth hands are on the cloth, so there is no hand free for a phone. q-app lives on a tablet on a stand at the station, and works on phones for everyone who moves.
  2. the common case costs one tapmost pieces pass. passing is one very large button, hit without looking and without putting the piece down.
  3. the exception costs a piece numberto reject or alter, the stitching master enters the piece number first. the friction is uneven on purpose: data is only asked for where it is worth having, and every defect is tied to a piece and a line.
  4. alter is not a failurethree states, not two. most stitching defects can be repaired, and a pass-or-fail control would force people to either lie or throw away good work.

built for the people using it

eight native languages

the words on the buttons are in the language of the person pressing them.

offline first

every tap is stored on the device and syncs as soon as the network returns. a dead signal never stops a line.

the factory sees itself

factories big enough to have tvs put q-app on them. the screen changes every 15 to 20 seconds: the stitching department, the cutting department, dhu graphs, today's defect count. the whole day's quality, live, where everyone on the floor can see it.

for floor managers and owners there are dashboards on their own devices, so they can see a line's performance from anywhere, reward the strong lines and retrain the weak ones. those dashboards came straight out of field research: i designed them on the floor, with the people who would use them.

how it landed

workmanship, meaning stitching and cutting, is the first audit in final inspection, and that is where q-app showed. defects there fell by roughly 30 to 40%. that is a range i remember from our dashboards, not a figure i can source, and there is no line-by-line before and after that survived.

what i can say without a number: a defect is now recorded at the moment it is found, against the piece and the line that made it, instead of being discovered in a finished shipment.

the best quality tool is the one the stitching master barely notices using.

observation 06

the best decision in the fashinza work is where he put the tablet.

both hands on the cloth, so the device goes on a stand. everything else in q-app follows from watching someone work, which is the evidence his fello file is missing.

prevention · designed from the floor

catch defects earlier.

a quality brief like that usually produces a longer form. a longer form on a stitching line is a form nobody fills in.

one tap to pass. a piece number to fail.

a tablet on a stand, one enormous pass button, reject and alter behind a piece number, three states instead of two, eight languages, offline storage, and the day's quality rotating on the factory's own tvs.

friction was the design material.

most people spread friction evenly and call it consistency. he put none on the common case and a little on the exception, which is exactly where the data is worth having.

and alter, the third state, is the kind of decision that only comes from standing on a line: most defects are repairable, and a binary control would teach people to lie.

my read, not his numbers. users score high because every choice is fitted to a body at a workstation. business is held down because the only outcome figure is a remembered range.

he has met the user. many of them.

the strongest objection to his fello work is that it is systems-first. this is the counter-evidence: a hundred-plus factories, eight languages, posture, network, and a tv on a factory wall.

it is not consumer mobile. it is the closest thing to it on this site, and in some ways harder.

the honest caveats.

30 to 40% fewer workmanship defects is a range he remembers from dashboards, not a figure he can source, and he says exactly that. there is no line-level before and after.

100+ factories is reach, not depth of use. ask him what adoption looked like on a forty-line floor versus a three-line one.
