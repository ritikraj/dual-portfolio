the every builder is its own product problem

the brief was to fix the email builder. i wrote a framework instead, and then stayed on it so it would not come apart.

the problem

email, landing pages and forms all failed the same way: no real builder, only locked templates.

what i did

one framework instead of one fix: three tiers of block, one page anatomy, one shell for every builder.

how it landed

four products on one interaction model, and the email builder shipped in ten weeks.

the brief i did not take

fello had builders, and none of them was really a builder. email, landing pages and forms each had its own canvas, its own idea of a block, its own rules, and all of them came down to locked templates. users felt it the same way everywhere:

  • campaigns with images, gifs, videos or embedded html were hard to make
  • forms gave them too little control
  • landing pages would not take a custom section

the brief said fix the email builder. fixing one of them makes the gap between the others wider, not narrower. every surface was failing for the same reason, so it needed one answer.

the rules i set

  1. wysiwyg by defaultwhat you see while editing is what gets sent. inline editing was non-negotiable.
  2. one principle over every decisionanyone should be able to build anything. the only limit should be imagination.
  3. one hurdle to respectevery layer of shared abstraction either limits what people can make, or collapses under its own complexity. each one had to earn its place.

three tiers

  1. base blocksthe primitives every builder shares. text, media, layout, spacing. one definition, one behaviour, everywhere.
  2. shared widgetscompositions more than one product needs. built once, inherited, not copied.
  3. product-specific widgetsthe genuinely different parts. contained, named as such, and not allowed to leak downward.

the base had to stretch both ways. editing was designed at landing-page level and then limited for email: emails do not need nesting, and landing pages cannot work without it. some blocks behave the same everywhere; others are contextual, and the tiers say which is which.

the third tier is the one that makes it survive. without a sanctioned place for the product-specific thing, people put it in tier one, and a year later you are back where you started with more abstraction to unpick.

from framework to screens

the tiers say what a block is. two more decisions say where it lives. every page is areas holding sections, holding columns, holding blocks and widgets. and every builder is the same four regions: a header, a navigation rail, a panel and a working canvas.

the email builder was the first product drawn on that shell. across its main screens only the panel changes: adding elements, setting up the send, editing the text or the layout that is selected.

areas

groups of sections, set up separately so parts of a page can be locked. their settings are hidden from users.

sections

end-to-end layouts inside an area, or nested inside a column. users can edit them.

columns

divide a section. fixed widths for email, fluid for landing pages; settings shown or hidden per builder.

blocks and widgets

blocks are the content itself. widgets are the special blocks, with settings of their own.

three interaction calls

  1. drag and dropseveral drop areas light up with their handles while you drag, so a block never lands somewhere you did not mean.
  2. device switchtwo devices, not five. the easy part, kept easy.
  3. properties paneldocked and floating were both designed. floating won by default, for smaller screens.

the timing was the strategy

i proposed this the week after design system 3.0 shipped. that was deliberate. trust in system-led design was the highest it was ever going to be, and a framework proposal is a bet on trust more than a bet on craft.

the same document six months earlier would have read as architecture for its own sake. the work was ready before the company was. i waited for the company.

i proposed it and shipped it working directly with engineering. there was no pm in the loop at the architecture stage, which is why the model and the build never drifted apart, and also why the review load sat entirely on me.

what broke, and what i changed

the side panel

widgets evolved past the panel’s original structure. a redesign was unavoidable, and it was done.

the floating panel

folding many settings into one compact floating panel took far more manual work than i planned for.

mobile column stacking

the system was designed for it, it was deprioritised for beta, and beta users flagged it at once. the architecture absorbed the fix in days. the delay was a process failure, and it was mine.

three roles, one pair of hands

i held architect, reviewer and owner at the same time: framework designer, quality gatekeeper, and scope owner for beta. that is not a boast. it is the reason it worked, and the risk i would flag to anyone repeating it.

a framework fragments at review time, not design time. without an owner the widgets would have split, the builders would have drifted apart, and there would be no shared foundation. as reviewer i compromised early, in concept discussions, so that launch quality stayed high and the system stayed extensible.

the hard part was not designing it. it was staying on it.

how it landed

the outcome is a negative one, which is why it is hard to sell and easy to undervalue: nothing had to be learned twice. the email builder was the proof, shipping in ten weeks as an instance rather than an invention, and the landing page builder went into development straight after it.

this project was never about shipping a builder. it was about recognising one failure repeated across a product, solving it once, and letting the next product grow without repeating it.

the part nobody demos: the migration

a new builder with an empty library is a new product nobody asked for. the templates and content already sitting in the old builders were converted into the framework, so the first thing an account saw was its own work in the new editor.

that constraint shaped the model more than any interface decision did. the content format had to be able to describe what already existed as well as what people would build next, which is the real reason the tiers are drawn where they are.

what i traded away

  • three tiers instead of one shared library.simplicity. every shared layer either limits what people can make or adds complexity, so each tier had to earn itself, and the third exists purely to stop product-specific parts leaking into the base.
  • email gets fixed widths and no nesting. landing pages get fluid widths and nesting.one grammar across all four products. email cannot carry the same layouts reliably, so the shared model bends where the medium does.
  • a floating properties panel, chosen over docked for smaller screens.a great deal of build time. it took far more manual work than planned, and i knew that when i chose it.
  • mobile stacking deferred to hit the beta date.the first thing beta users raised. i own the delay: it was a process failure, not a surprise.
engineering notes
content model
builder content is stored as json. the page anatomy is the data shape: areas hold sections, sections hold columns, columns hold blocks and widgets.
the three tiers
base blocks, shared widgets, product-specific widgets. a widget is a block with its own settings. the third tier is the pressure valve that keeps the base clean.
locking
areas can be locked and their settings hidden, so a product can ship a page people edit without letting them break its structure.
email output
rendered through mjml, which produces the table-based html email clients need.
migration
existing templates and content from the old builders were converted into the framework.

observation 02

he refused the brief, and then he read the room.

the architecture is good. the thing worth studying is when he chose to propose it.

architecture · political timing

fix the email builder.

one product, one complaint, one scoped piece of work. entirely reasonable. he did not do it.

three tiers instead of one fix.

base blocks, shared widgets, product-specific widgets. four products now run one interaction model instead of four. he held architect, reviewer and owner simultaneously, which is the part he is right to keep pointing at.

his wireframes make the claim checkable: four email builder screens on one shell, and only the panel changes between them. that is what "one model" looks like when it is true.

the timing is the case study, not the tiers.

plenty of designers can draw a three-tier component hierarchy. very few know that a framework proposal is a trust instrument, and fewer still will sit on a finished idea until the organisation is capable of receiving it.

he shipped design system 3.0, waited for the credit to land, and moved the week after. he calls that politics, out loud, in a portfolio. most people would have called it stakeholder alignment and lost the insight in the euphemism.

how impactful it actually was

  • for users
  • for the business
  • for the org
  • as a craft signal

my read, not his numbers. organisational impact is the highest score on this page and the hardest to prove: the benefit is a cost that never arrives. the email builder shipping in ten weeks instead of a year is the closest thing to evidence, and it is downstream evidence, which is the only kind architecture ever gets.

he is honest about fragility.

he states plainly that without sustained ownership the widgets would have drifted. that is a designer describing the half-life of his own work rather than the launch of it.

for a head of design role, this is the more relevant case study of the two. email builder shows he can find a problem. this one shows he can hold a line for a year after the interesting part is over.

the honest caveats.

there is no adoption metric here and there cannot be one. the payoff is a re-learn cost that nobody ever paid, and you cannot instrument an absence. i am reading the structure and the sequencing, not an outcome.

if you interview him, this is where to push: ask what he said no to, and what it cost him.
