the brief i did not take

fello had builders. a landing page builder, an email builder, and more coming. each one had been built by whoever needed it, at the moment they needed it, with its own canvas, its own idea of a block, its own state rules.

to a user moving across the product, that is four products wearing one logo. every move costs them a re-learn. the brief said fix one of them. fixing one of them makes the gap between the four wider, not narrower.

three tiers

  1. base blocksthe primitives every builder shares. text, media, layout, spacing. one definition, one behaviour, everywhere.
  2. shared widgetscompositions that more than one product needs. built once, inherited, not copied.
  3. product-specific widgetsthe genuinely different parts. contained, named as such, and not allowed to leak downward.

the third tier is the one that makes it survive. without a sanctioned place to put the genuinely product-specific thing, people put it in tier one, and a year later you are back where you started with more abstraction to unpick.

the timing was the strategy

i proposed this the week after design system 3.0 shipped. that was deliberate. institutional trust in system-led design was the highest it was ever going to be, and a framework proposal is a bet on trust more than a bet on craft.

the same document, six months earlier, would have read as architecture for its own sake. the work was ready before the company was. i waited for the company.

the hard part was not designing it. it was staying on it.

three roles, one pair of hands

i held architect, reviewer and owner at the same time, and that is not a boast. it is the reason it worked, and it is also the risk i would flag to anyone repeating it.

a framework fragments at review time, not at design time. someone has to say no to the twentieth reasonable-sounding exception, week after week, long after the launch that made it exciting. remove sustained ownership and the widgets drift within two quarters.

observation 02

he refused the brief, and then he read the room.

the architecture is good. the thing worth studying is when he chose to propose it.

architecture · political timing

fix the email builder.

one product, one complaint, one scoped piece of work. entirely reasonable. he did not do it.

three tiers instead of one fix.

base blocks, shared widgets, product-specific widgets. four products now run one interaction model instead of four. he held architect, reviewer and owner simultaneously, which is the part he is right to keep pointing at.

the timing is the case study, not the tiers.

plenty of designers can draw a three-tier component hierarchy. very few know that a framework proposal is a trust instrument, and fewer still will sit on a finished idea until the organisation is capable of receiving it.

he shipped design system 3.0, waited for the credit to land, and moved the week after. he calls that politics, out loud, in a portfolio. most people would have called it stakeholder alignment and lost the insight in the euphemism.

my read, not his numbers. organisational impact is the highest score on this page and the hardest to prove: the benefit is a cost that never arrives. the email builder shipping in ten weeks instead of a year is the closest thing to evidence, and it is downstream evidence, which is the only kind architecture ever gets.

he is honest about fragility.

he states plainly that without sustained ownership the widgets would have drifted. that is a designer describing the half-life of his own work rather than the launch of it.

for a head of design role, this is the more relevant case study of the two. email builder shows he can find a problem. this one shows he can hold a line for a year after the interesting part is over.

the honest caveats.

there is no adoption metric here and there cannot be one. the payoff is a re-learn cost that nobody ever paid, and you cannot instrument an absence. i am reading the structure and the sequencing, not an outcome.

if you interview him, this is where to push: ask what he said no to, and what it cost him.
