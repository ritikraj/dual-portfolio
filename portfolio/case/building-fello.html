the design that doesn't survive me problem

i arrived owning features someone else had scoped, and left owning the foundation the platform stands on. the part worth reading is what happened in between, including the parts that did not work.

what i inherited

two features that were already in flight, and neither of them is still doing its job. an automation budget that let owners cap what their automations could spend, which worked exactly as designed right up until the company stopped charging credits and the whole problem it solved disappeared underneath it. and a notification settings rework that got pushed backwards more than once until what shipped was thinner than what we started with.

i am leading with those on purpose. they are the first thing a design lead should be able to talk about, and the last thing most portfolios mention.

why they did not survive

neither was a craft failure, and i am not going to pretend otherwise to look humble. they were symptoms of a company that had not finished deciding what it was. you cannot design a durable budget control for a pricing model that is about to be withdrawn, and you cannot win a scope argument about notifications while the roadmap is still moving under everyone.

i lost the notifications argument. twice. the second time i stopped arguing and went to find out why i kept losing it.

the honest lesson is that i had been arguing from taste and from the shape of the interface, which is the weakest ground a designer can stand on. the research came after the losses, not before them.

how the turn got earned

nobody handed me the platform. what changed it was going deeper into the evidence than anyone else was going: sitting in support tickets, sales calls and usage data until i could describe the product's problems more precisely than the people who owned them. an opinion loses to a roadmap. a synthesis nobody else has does not.

that is the whole mechanism, and it is transferable. the email builder and the builder framework both came out of it.

what leading seven actually meant

  1. critique, weekly, with teethwork got reviewed against the system and the evidence, not against my preferences. the point of a standing critique is that the lead stops being the bottleneck on quality.
  2. hiringi sat on the other side of the table and had to learn to describe what good looked like in words that survived me not being there.
  3. system governancesomeone has to say no to the twentieth reasonable exception. that is unglamorous, it never ships, and it is most of why the design system held.
  4. design ops and handoff standardsthe boring plumbing that decides whether a design survives the distance between figma and production.

none of that is visible in a portfolio shot. it is visible in whether the work still looks like itself two quarters after you stop watching it.

observation 05

the one that decides whether he leads a team.

the least interesting brief there is, and the one most designers spend their first year on.

he ended up owning the foundation instead.

thirty months, a team of seven, and the whole unglamorous apparatus around it: weekly critique, hiring, system governance, design ops, handoff standards. the title arrived after the work rather than before it, which is the order that tells you something.

he opens with what he lost.

an automation budget made obsolete by a pricing change, and a notifications rework he argued for twice and lost twice. he does not dress either up as a learning experience. he says the arguments were lost because he was arguing from taste and interface shape, which is the weakest ground available, and that the research came after the losses rather than before them.

that is a specific, unflattering, structurally useful admission. i have read a lot of portfolios. i have not read that sentence before.

my read, not his numbers. organisational impact is close to the ceiling and almost entirely unverifiable from outside, which is true of every leadership claim ever made. what can be checked is downstream: the design system's detach rate and the builder framework's survival both depend on the governance described here.

he describes the half-life of his own influence.

his closing metric is what keeps working once he is no longer there to defend it. designers do not usually think in those terms. it implies he builds for the org that continues without him rather than for the credit that accrues to him, and it is the single strongest argument on this site for putting him above a team.

the critique cadence is the other tell. a weekly review held against the system and the evidence, rather than against the lead's preferences, is a deliberate move to stop being the bottleneck on quality. that is a leader building capacity, not a senior designer marking homework.

the honest caveats.

everything on this page is a claim about how a person behaved inside a building i cannot see into. the team size, the critique cadence, the hiring, the two lost arguments: all of it is his account. none of it has a number attached that an outsider could audit, and it would be dishonest of me to score it as though it did.

so take this one to references rather than to the page. ask the seven. that is the only version of this case study that counts.
