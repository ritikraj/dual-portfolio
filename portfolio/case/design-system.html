the problem

what existed was a style guide with about twenty components, no documentation and no design language behind it. the stated problem was speed: design was the queue everything waited in.

for the team

no reusable code, so engineering rebuilt the same things. no ux architecture to lean on, no ui consistency, and nothing ready for ai.

for users

new features needed help to find. the product relied on recall instead of recognition, and people relearned it every few weeks.

it was the eleventh hour. every new builder, workflow or ai feature would compound the inconsistency, and the cost of change was rising faster than feature velocity. a ux audit mapped every component the product would need.

the problem under the problem

the company was mid-identity-change. it had sold itself as a marketing tool and was becoming an ai-first real estate operating system. a system built for the first would need tearing out within a year of the second arriving. deciding which company i was building for was the real first decision, and it was not a design decision.

so every component was engineered to hold ai before engineering wrote a line of ai: ai variants on cards, loaders and graphs, and headers tested with the ai elements present and absent, so neither state was the special case. it looks like over-engineering right up until the roadmap turns.

a strong design system is 80% thinking and 20% figma.

how i built it, in order

  1. principles firstatomic design as the method, and five principles for the whole product: scalable, simple, purposeful, unambiguous, empowering. plus one rule that saved months: a variation of an existing component before a new component.
  2. variables in three layersprimitives defined and locked. global variables defined and tested across real screens. component variables only where a global one failed. tokens for colour, spacing, type and elevation, and a fluid column layout from day one.
  3. skeletons before components200+ iterations of the main pages to settle the taste, then skeletons for every other page. the base and advanced components were read off those skeletons, not guessed at.
  4. the base layerbuttons, inputs, loaders, tooltips: the parts that rarely change. accessibility was built in from the start and solved with global variables, so no base component needed variables of its own.
  5. the architectureproduct-wide patterns, on logic similar to slack, hubspot and google chat: top navbar and main sidebar, page and section headers, cards and a robust table cell, secondary navigation panels, modals, bottomsheets, side popups, empty states and reusable illustrations.

the architecture, and the hand-off

token architecture in figma variables, carrying multi-theme and multi-context from the start rather than retrofitting either. 120+ coded components against 1,000+ figma components, with accessibility audited to wcag 2.1 aa rather than asserted. i benchmarked against atlassian, polaris, lightning and spectrum, to know exactly which of their decisions i was declining, and why.

the design took the team about ten to twelve weeks. engineering did not wait for it: they started in parallel, early, building the components and frameworks each flow would need, and the work was split so every designer owned a module.

how it landed

<1%detach rate
120+coded components
3.6Minsertions in 6 months
~30%faster to delivery
  • 100% of new product surfaces built on it after launch
  • zero new custom components outside the system for the next nine months
  • three designers onboarded and shipping inside it on their own within a week
  • the ai variants needed no retrofitting when the ai features shipped
  • it made the builder architecture possible, and gave contacts, builders, workflows and reports one visual language

3.6 million insertions says the system was used. the detach rate says whether it was trusted, and it is the number i actually watch. adoption can be mandated. a detach rate under one percent means designers stopped fighting the system to do their job.

what was hard

the iterations

200+ page iterations took several designers weeks before the ideas were ready to show anyone outside design.

the skeletons

they worked for designers. everyone else struggled to picture the finished ui from a skeleton screen.

the names

variable nomenclature clashed between design and engineering, again and again.

the constraints

designers struggled to create inside the system, and every component needed real thought about how it would scale, adapt and behave.

the qa loops

hand-off friction meant tight qa cycles. qa retested the whole product, because the entire design language was new to them.

the tenth one

i have built ten or more design systems. most of them failed, and they failed the same way: built as a component library, handed over as a component library, quietly abandoned when the product changed shape.

this one worked because the thinking went in before the file did, and because governance was treated as part of the design rather than as admin that happens afterwards.

you have read my account. there is another one.

how impactful it actually was

  • for users
  • for the business
  • for the org
  • as a craft signal

my read, not his numbers. user impact is the lowest here and that is not a criticism: a design system reaches users through other people’s work. organisational impact is close to the ceiling. 3.6 million insertions says it was used; the detach rate under one percent says it was trusted, and that is still the single most credible number on his site.

he watches the right number.

adoption can be mandated. a detach rate under one percent cannot. he picked the metric that could embarrass him, which tells you he was trying to learn something rather than trying to report something.

and he is straight about the other nine attempts that failed. ten design systems, one that stuck, and he leads with the ratio rather than burying it.

the honest caveats.

~30% faster design-to-delivery is the softest figure in his portfolio. delivery speed has a dozen inputs and a design system is one of them. i would treat it as directionally true and not as an attribution.

3.6 million insertions in six months is real usage, but it is a volume number. it grows with headcount and with how many screens a team draws, not with how good the system is. read it next to the detach rate, never instead of it.

the detach rate, the component counts and the aa audit are all checkable inside the company. those are the ones to ask about.
